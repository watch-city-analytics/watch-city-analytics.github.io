It started with a town that could not afford to compete.

Not a market analysis. A youth sports problem that turned out to be everyone's problem.

A few years ago the question in front of us was narrow and practical: how does a community that cannot afford the dominant video platform give its kids the same shot as the town down the road?

The answer at the time was that it could not. Film study, opponent scouting, the breakdown that tells a fourteen-year-old what they are actually doing with their feet — all of it sat behind a subscription that some programs could absorb and others could not. The programs that could afford it got better. The ones that could not fell further behind, and the gap compounded every season.

That is a familiar shape once you start looking for it. A capability becomes standard, the price of entry sits just above what a small organization can justify, and a slow sorting happens between the places that can pay and the places that cannot.

Then it became clear what was possible once AI was applied to the problem. Not as a product to sell, but as a way to rebuild the capability from parts a town could actually afford — open models and commercial ones, the cloud services schools already pay for and barely use, and a lot of careful engineering in between.

The analysis turned out not to be the expensive part. The expensive part was that somebody else owned it.

That is the firm. We build the capability a small organization is being priced out of, on infrastructure it controls, so the advantage stays where the work is done.

Access is the whole point

Large enterprises have architects. They have people whose job is to ask what happens to the data, what the second-year cost looks like, and what it takes to leave a vendor. A school district, a town office, a family business and a club program do not have those people, and they are sold to as though they do.

We bring that bench. Our work pairs industry veterans — people who ran operations, finance, athletics and property before they worked in AI — with architects who have built production data systems at enterprise scale. The result is the kind of expertise that normally only arrives with an enterprise budget, aimed at organizations that have never had access to it.

That reach matters more than headcount. We can put the right specialist on a problem without asking a client to fund a discovery phase while somebody learns their business.

Why we are difficult about data

This part is not a marketing position. Our founder has spent more than twenty-five years designing storage and data architecture at enterprise scale — through EMC and Dell, and alongside partners building modern object storage platforms. The entire discipline is about where data lives, who can reach it, what it costs to keep, and how you get it back out.

Which means we recognize vendor lock-in on sight. We know what it looks like when a platform's pricing is structured around your archive rather than your usage, when an export is technically available and practically useless, and when a contract quietly makes your own operating history the vendor's asset.

So we design the other way. Your data in your storage, under your control, in formats you can move. Models chosen per workload — commercial where it earns its cost, open-weight where you need to hold it yourself — and swappable when something better arrives. Nothing we build should be difficult to take away from us.

The name

Watch City is Waltham, Massachusetts, where American watchmaking industrialized a craft that had been done one bench at a time. The part worth remembering is what the factory kept. Precision did not come from replacing the watchmakers. It came from giving people who understood the mechanism better instruments.

That is the work.
