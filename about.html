It started with a town that could not afford to compete.

Not a market analysis. A youth sports problem that turned out to be everyone's problem.

A few years ago the question in front of us was narrow and practical: how does a community that cannot afford the dominant video platform give its kids the same shot as the town down the road?

The answer at the time was that it could not. Film study, opponent scouting, the breakdown that tells a fourteen-year-old what they are actually doing with their feet — all of it sat behind a subscription that some programs could absorb and others could not. The programs that could afford it got better. The ones that could not fell further behind, and the gap compounded every season.

A capability becomes standard, the price of entry sits just above what a small organization can justify, and a slow sorting happens between the places that can pay and the places that cannot. It is a familiar shape once you start looking for it.

Then it became clear what was possible once AI was applied to the problem. Not as a product to sell, but as a way to rebuild the thing out of parts a town could actually afford — the cloud services schools already pay for and barely use, whichever AI system suited the job, and a great deal of careful engineering in between.

The analysis turned out not to be the expensive part. The expensive part was that somebody else owned it.

That is the firm. We build the capability a small organization is being priced out of, on infrastructure it controls, so the advantage stays where the work is done.

A bench, assembled one introduction at a time

Large enterprises have architects. They have people whose job is to ask what happens to the data, what the year-three cost looks like, and what it takes to leave a vendor. A town office, a school district, a college business office, a non-profit, a small business and a club program do not have those people, and they are sold to as though they do.

This practice exists to be those people for organizations that will never have them on staff. It began with one career and grew by introduction: thirty years of building systems alongside operators, architects, designers and builders who were the best in the room at their own trade. When the firm needed someone, it did not advertise. It called people it had already worked with.

The engagement is fractional, and that is the whole economic trick. A town office cannot hire a data architect, and should not want to — it would be idle eleven months of the year. What it needs is that architect for three weeks in March. So that is what we sell: the right senior specialist for the fraction of time the work genuinely takes, chosen by name, introduced to you, and gone when the job is done. You get the expertise an enterprise budget buys, sized to a problem rather than to a payroll.

What holds it together is a method rather than an org chart, and the method is Sanjay’s. Start with one job. Go and watch it being done before anybody proposes anything. Put a number on what it costs in hours. Build on infrastructure the client already owns, documented well enough that they could replace us. Everyone who works here works that way — it is the condition of working here at all.

Which does not mean everybody here has thirty years behind them, and it should not. The people who came up with these tools already in their hands read them faster than any veteran does. They know what the thing can actually be made to do, and the shortcut to making it do it. What they do not have is the decades of watching an industry work that tell you what is worth building in the first place.

What you are promised, then, is not an absence of young people. It is that the senior who scoped your job is accountable for it from the first conversation to the last, by name, and that you are never handed off.

Who you are dealing with

Watch City Analytics was founded by Sanjay Patel, who grew up in Waltham — the watch city itself — and has spent thirty years building IT systems, nearly three decades of them at EMC and Dell.

He studied network engineering at the University of Massachusetts Amherst, joined EMC in 1999 as a storage systems engineer, and stayed on through its acquisition by Dell. Recent years have been spent on storage architecture at the largest scale it is done at.

Along the way he spent years building with partners at both ends of the scale, from two-person shops to companies with a hundred thousand employees.

What a working method looks like, and what a failing one looks like earlier than most people see it. You watch a buyer decide. You watch what they were promised. Then you watch what actually happens in their building six months later. The same failure repeats: an organization buys a system, trains nobody, and the afternoon comes back. It is not a technology problem and it was never fixed by another purchase. It is fixed by watching the work before anybody proposes anything, which is why that is step one here and why it is free.

The people who need this most are the ones nobody builds for.

A two-person town office. A district business manager with a June deadline. A family business that runs on one person's memory. A volunteer coach at the kitchen table on a Sunday night. They are expert at what they do — they have simply never had anyone in the room whose job was to ask the awkward questions on their behalf. That is the bias this firm runs on, and it is the reason the work is priced and scoped the way it is.

Why we are obsessed about where your data lives

Data is a cold word for something you already know intimately. The permit files. The purchase orders. The game film. Forty years of correspondence in a cabinet behind the desk. Everything your organization has written down, and everything it has quietly learned along the way. We call it data because the software industry does. You probably call it the records, or the paperwork, or just the files.

It is the one thing we will not let go of. Underneath every system sit the same four questions: where your data actually lives, who can get at it, what it costs to keep it there, and what it would take to get it back out. Most organizations only ask the fourth one when it is already too late.

Which means we recognize a trap when we see one. A price that climbs with how much of your own history you are allowed to keep. An export button that technically works and hands you something nobody can use. A contract that quietly turns the record of how you operate into somebody else's property.

So we design the other way. Your records in your own storage, under your control, in a form you can pick up and move. The AI system chosen to suit the job — sometimes one you buy by the use, sometimes one that runs on your own side where it has to — and swapped out when something better arrives. Nothing we build should be difficult to take away from us.

The name

Watch City is Waltham, Massachusetts, where American watchmaking industrialized a craft that had been done one bench at a time. The factories did not replace the watchmakers. They took people who understood the mechanism and could never make enough of them, and gave them machines that could — and that industrialization is a fair part of what built this country.

That is what the name is for, because the same thing is true again. AI is the next one of those. It is not here to replace the people who know the work; it is here to let them do more of it than a pair of hands was ever going to manage.

What is different this time is who gets left out. The last industrial revolution reached the people who could build a factory. This one can reach a two-person town office, a volunteer coach and a six-man crew — but only if somebody bothers to bring it to them.

That is the work.
