It started with a town whose youth sports teams could not afford to compete.

Not a market analysis. A youth sports problem that turned out to be everyone's problem.

A few years ago the question in front of us was narrow and practical: youth sports run on video now, and the software that makes that work costs more than some towns can pay. How does a community that cannot afford it give its kids the same shot as the town down the road?

The answer at the time was that it could not. Coaches, most of them volunteers, study video all week: their own games, the team they play next, and the clip that finally tells a fourteen-year-old what they are actually doing with their feet. All of it sat behind a subscription that some programs could absorb and others could not. The programs that could afford it got better. The ones that could not fell further behind, and the gap compounded every season.

That is a familiar shape once you start looking for it. A capability becomes standard, the price of entry sits just above what a small organization can justify, and a slow sorting happens between the places that can pay and the places that cannot.

Then it became clear what was possible once AI was applied to the problem. Not as a product to sell, but as a way to rebuild the thing out of parts a town could actually afford — the cloud services schools already pay for and barely use, whichever AI system suited the job, and a great deal of careful engineering in between.

The analysis turned out not to be the expensive part. The expensive part was that somebody else owned it.

That is the firm. We build the capability a small organization is being priced out of, on infrastructure it controls, so the advantage stays where the work is done.

A bench, assembled one introduction at a time

Large enterprises have architects. They have people whose job is to ask what happens to the data, what the second-year cost looks like, and what it takes to leave a vendor. A town office, a school district, a college business office, a non-profit, a small business and a club program do not have those people, and they are sold to as though they do.

This practice exists to be those people for organizations that will never have one on staff. It began with one career and grew by introduction: thirty years of building systems alongside operators, architects, designers and builders who were the best in the room at their own trade. When the firm needed someone, it did not advertise. It called people it had already worked with.

A bench you can only build by spending thirty years earning it.

The engagement is fractional, and that is the whole economic trick. A town office cannot hire a data architect, and should not want to — it would be idle eleven months of the year. What it needs is that architect for three weeks in March. So that is what we sell: the right senior specialist for the fraction of time the work genuinely takes, chosen by name, introduced to you, and gone when the job is done. You get the expertise an enterprise budget buys, sized to a problem rather than to a payroll.

What holds it together is a method rather than an org chart, and the method is Sanjay’s. Start with one job. Go and watch it being done before anybody proposes anything. Put a number on what it costs in hours. Build on infrastructure the client already owns, documented well enough that they could replace us. Everyone who works here works that way — it is the condition of working here at all.

Which means you should never be the client who gets the junior. There are no juniors. There is a person who has done this for twenty or thirty years, for the few weeks you need them.

Who you are dealing with

Watch City Analytics was founded by Sanjay Patel, who grew up in Waltham — the watch city itself — and has spent more than thirty years building IT systems, nearly three decades of that at EMC and Dell.

He studied network engineering at the University of Massachusetts Amherst, joined EMC in 1999 as a storage systems engineer, and stayed on through its acquisition by Dell. Recent years have been spent on storage architecture for the biggest cloud operators in the world, alongside advising companies building the tools that take the hand-work out of the job.

But the job description has never really been the technology. It has been sitting with people who know their own business, working out what they are actually trying to accomplish, and then deciding what should be built — and, as often, what should not be. Thirty years of that is thirty years of watching the expensive failure happen the same way every time: somebody buys the system first and discovers the business need afterwards.

None of which is why this firm exists.

It exists because of a youth football program in a town that could not afford what the town down the road had bought. Watching a local program fall behind for want of a subscription, the question stopped being a technical one. Thirty years of translating between what an organization needs and what technology can actually do turned out to be exactly what a small organization needed, and had never once been offered to one.

The people who need this most are the ones nobody builds for.

A two-person town office. A district business manager with a June deadline. A family business that runs on one person's memory. A volunteer coach at the kitchen table on a Sunday night. They are expert at what they do — they have simply never had anyone in the room whose job was to ask the awkward questions on their behalf. That is the bias this firm runs on, and it is the reason the work is priced and scoped the way it is.

Why we are obsessed about where your data lives

Data is a cold word for something you already know intimately. The permit files. The purchase orders. The game film. Forty years of correspondence in a cabinet behind the desk. Everything your organization has written down, and everything it has quietly learned along the way. We call it data because the software industry does. You probably call it the records, or the paperwork, or just the files.

It is the one thing we will not let go of, and it comes straight out of those thirty years. Underneath every system sit the same four questions: where does your data actually live, who can get at it, what does it cost to keep it there, and what would it take to get it back out. Most organizations only ask the fourth one when it is already too late.

Which means we recognize a trap when we see one. A price that climbs with how much of your own history you are allowed to keep. An export button that technically works and hands you something nobody can use. A contract that quietly turns the record of how you operate into somebody else's property.

So we design the other way. Your records in your own storage, under your control, in a form you can pick up and move. The AI system chosen to suit the job — sometimes one you buy by the use, sometimes one that runs on your own side where it has to — and swapped out when something better arrives. Nothing we build should be difficult to take away from us.

The name

Watch City is Waltham, Massachusetts, where American watchmaking industrialized a craft that had been done one bench at a time. The part worth remembering is what the factory kept. Precision did not come from replacing the watchmakers. It came from giving people who understood the mechanism better instruments.

That is the work.
