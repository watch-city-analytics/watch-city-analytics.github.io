The arrangement nobody designed but everybody accepted

One platform now sits between most programs and their own film. It handles exchange with opponents, it holds the archive, and it sells the breakdown back. The published tiers for a single high school program run $1,500, $2,500 and $4,000 a year, and what separates them is mostly how many hours of your own history you are allowed to keep.

Districts pay a good deal more than that. One Oklahoma district signed for nine high schools at $139,500 a year — $15,500 per school. A Montana district’s board packet last June recorded the renewal going from $8,900 to $11,200, a rise of nearly twenty-six per cent in a single year. These are not list prices off a website; they are figures in public board minutes, and your own renewal letter is the only one that matters.

What the storage underneath actually costs. A season of game, scout and practice film for one high school football program is roughly seventy-five hours, or about 225 gigabytes. Held for a year in ordinary cloud storage that is somewhere between $19 and $58, depending on the provider and how much of it gets watched. Even the largest published tier’s 750-hour allowance costs under $600 a year to store at list rates.

We are not going to tell you that makes it a rip-off. Most of what you are paying for is the breakdown labour, the turnaround time, the apps, and the exchange network — and those are real. The honest point is narrower and more useful: when a tier upgrade is put in front of you as a storage limit, storage is not what you are being asked to buy.

You do not have to leave. You have to stop paying for the part you can own.

What the agreement says about your film

We went and read it, because almost nobody does. Three things are worth knowing, and we have tried to state them exactly rather than dramatically.

You own the film. The organization agreement says so plainly. The base license the platform takes is narrow — it can use your material to the extent needed to provide you the service.

Two carve-outs widen that considerably. Practice video run through the platform’s own breakdown tools carries a perpetual, irrevocable license for the company to use it in developing and improving its products. Film placed into opponent exchange carries a perpetual license for the company to derive data from it, and that video is added to the library available to users of its products. Both survive the end of your subscription.

There is no export clause. We looked for one specifically. The organization terms and the master subscription agreement contain no obligation to return your material, in any format, within any window. What they do say is that on termination your access is switched off. You can get video out while you are a customer — selected clips, as MP4 — but the tagging and breakdown that made it useful does not appear to travel with it.

We will not tell you your film is training somebody’s AI, because the agreement covering school film does not say that, and we would rather be the firm that read it properly than the one with the better headline.

How this actually works

Nobody cancels anything. There is no migration, no fight with your league, and no week where your staff cannot exchange film.

i

Keep the platform for what only it can do

Exchange with opponents and recruiting profiles are network effects. No one can replicate them and we will not pretend otherwise. Those stay exactly where they are.

ii

Move your archive somewhere you control

This is the step that makes the rest possible, because the expensive tiers are mostly buying you storage. Your film goes into storage your organization owns, at a cost that does not climb because somebody repriced a plan.

iii

Drop to the tier that covers what is left

For most programs the premium tiers exist to deliver breakdown and storage. Once those are handled, the entry tier does the remaining job. The difference is real money, and it is subtraction rather than a projection.

iv

Run the breakdown on your own stack

Your tagging scheme, your tendencies, the questions your staff actually asks. Built on AI that runs against your film, in your account, training nobody else's system.

The saving funds the work. What you get back is analysis your opponents do not have.
