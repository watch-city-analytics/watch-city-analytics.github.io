Four industries with nothing in common. The same method each time.

Which is the point. What we bring is not a product — it is a way of taking a process apart and deciding honestly which parts a machine should touch.

Athletics — the town that could not afford to compete

A youth football program with volunteer coaches, no budget for the dominant film platform, and opponents who had it. The gap showed up on Friday nights and compounded every season.

The problem. Film study is the cheapest competitive advantage in coaching and the one most out of reach. Breakdown sat behind a subscription that some programs absorbed and others could not. The coaches who could not pay were doing it by hand on Sunday evenings, or not at all.

What we built. A capture-to-analysis pipeline running on cloud services the organization already had access to. Film goes in, play-level breakdown comes out — formation, personnel, tendencies — with the archive held in storage the program controls rather than rented by the hour. It runs alongside whatever platform a league requires for exchange, so nothing has to be cancelled and no league rule is broken.

Why it mattered. The Sunday evening came back. And because the analysis runs on the program's own tagging rather than a platform's standard breakdown, it is not the same analysis the opponent receives.

Property — forty years of knowledge held in one head

A family property business running apartments and condominiums across two states. Decades of operating history, almost none of it written down anywhere a second person could use.

The problem. The operation ran on memory and paper. Which tenant called about what, when a unit was last turned and what it cost, which vendor to use for which building, when the tax and water bills fall due and what was paid last time. It worked because one person held all of it, which is also precisely the risk.

What we built. The records, correspondence and payment history brought into a form the business can actually ask questions of, with the recurring correspondence drafted rather than written from scratch each time. Nothing moved to a platform. The data sits in accounts the family owns.

Why it mattered. The knowledge stopped being a single point of failure. A business that had been one illness away from chaos became one somebody else could step into.

SECTORSmall and family-run business
THE CONSTRAINTNo IT function, no appetite for new software, and a hard requirement that the owner could still work the way he always had

Apparel — a clothing brand built by one person

A streetwear label taking a design from sketch to a product someone can buy, with a founder who is also the designer, the production manager, the storefront and the marketing department.

The problem. The constraint in a small brand is never ideas. It is that four jobs are being done by one person, and three of them are being done badly because there is no time left. Design iteration stalls because writing the product listings ate the evening.

What we built. AI into the production work rather than around it — pushing design variations quickly enough to actually choose between them, drafting product and listing copy that sounds like the brand instead of like a template, and generating the supporting assets a launch needs.

Why it mattered. It moved the bottleneck back to taste, which is where it belongs. The difference between a brand that launches and one that stays an idea is usually just whether the unglamorous half of the work got done.

SECTORApparel and consumer brands
THE CONSTRAINTA single founder carrying every function, and no budget to hire any of them out

Games — three titles shipped by a studio of one

An independent game studio building and releasing on a platform with millions of players, where the competition includes teams with actual payrolls.

The problem. Shipping a game means writing code, designing systems, making art, testing, and then doing the whole thing again when players find the edges. A solo developer's velocity is the entire business, and the hours lost to debugging are hours not spent on what makes the game good.

What we built. AI into the development loop — writing and debugging game code, working through systems design, and shortening the distance between an idea and something playable. Three titles have been released this way.

Why it mattered. Shipping three times is how you learn what works. A studio that can iterate at the pace of a larger team gets that many more attempts at being right.

SECTORIndependent game development
THE CONSTRAINTOne developer against studios with teams, where iteration speed is the only lever

What an engagement looks like elsewhere

We are built for the public sector and have not yet published work there. Rather than dress something up, here are two engagements described as they would actually run — illustrative, not accounts of work delivered.

A town clerk's office

Permit applications, licence renewals and records requests arriving by email, post and counter, each one re-keyed into a system that was specified in 2004. Two staff, neither of whom can take a holiday in the same week.

The assessment would measure what a single permit actually costs in staff minutes end to end, then test whether intake and triage can be handled before a person opens the file — leaving the determination, which is a judgement with legal weight, firmly with the clerk. Residents' data stays inside the town's own accounts throughout.

A district business office

Purchase orders, vendor contracts, grant reporting and the quarterly scramble to evidence how restricted funds were spent. One business manager, a fiscal year that ends in June, and a filing system that is partly a shared drive and partly a cabinet.

The assessment would look at grant reporting first, because it is the deadline that hurts most and the one where the source material already exists in writing. Student data would be scoped out entirely at the first conversation rather than discovered at signature.

If you recognise your own week in either of those, that is the conversation to have.
