Start with the week that looks like yours.

Six operations with almost nothing in common — a town office, a school business office, a coaching staff, a family property business, a clothing label and a game studio — and the same job underneath: work being done by hand that is eating people you cannot replace.

Municipal

A town office

Permit applications, license renewals and records requests arriving by email, by post and over the counter, each one typed again into a system that was specified in 2004. Two staff, and neither can take vacation the same week as the other.

We would start by timing what one permit really costs — every minute, from the envelope landing on the counter to the file being closed. Most offices have never seen that number, and the number on its own changes the conversation.

Then we would test whether the sorting and the typing can happen before anyone opens the file. The decision itself stays with the clerk, where the law puts it. Residents' details never leave the town's own accounts.

Schools and districts

A school business office

Purchase orders, vendor contracts, grant reporting, and the week in October spent proving where restricted money went — hunting receipts across a shared drive and a filing cabinet, with the deadline on Friday.

We would look at the grant reporting first, because it is the deadline that hurts most and the one where the source material already exists in writing.

We would agree in the first conversation that we do not touch student records at all. Written down then, not discovered at signature.

Where the method has already run

Four jobs we have actually done. Not one thing in common between them — except somebody doing four jobs and losing their evenings to the dullest one.

Youth and school athletics

The Sunday evening a coaching staff got back

A youth football program with volunteer coaches, no budget for the film platform everyone else uses, and opponents who had it. The gap showed up on Friday nights and compounded every season.

The problem. Film study is the cheapest advantage in coaching and the one most out of reach. The breakdown sat behind a subscription some programs could absorb and others could not. The coaches who could not pay were doing it by hand on Sunday evenings, at the kitchen table, or not at all.

What we built. The whole thing runs on the cloud account the organization already had and was barely using. Film goes in after the game. By morning every play is broken out — formation, who was on the field, what they tend to do on third down. The film itself sits in storage the program owns, not rented by the month. It runs alongside whatever platform a league requires, so nothing has to be cancelled and no league rule is broken.

What it gave back. The Sunday evening. And because the breakdown follows the coaches' own names for things rather than a standard report, it is not the same analysis the opponent receives.

Family-run business

Forty years in one man's head

A family property business running apartments and condominiums across two states. Decades of operating history, almost none of it written down anywhere a second person could use.

The problem. The operation ran on memory and paper. Which tenant called about what, when a unit was last turned and what it cost, which contractor to use for which building, when the tax and water bills fall due and what was paid last time. It worked because one person held all of it, which is also precisely the risk.

What we built. The records, the correspondence and the payment history brought into a form anyone in the office can simply ask — when did we last do this unit, and what did it cost? — and get the answer in seconds instead of an afternoon. The letters that follow a pattern get drafted rather than written from scratch. Nothing moved onto anybody's platform. The records sit in accounts the family owns.

What it gave back. A business that had been one illness away from chaos became one somebody else could step into. He still works the way he always has.

THE CONSTRAINTNo IT department, no appetite for new software, and a hard requirement that the owner could carry on exactly as before
Apparel and consumer brands

The brand that got its designer back

A clothing label taking a design from sketch to something a person can buy, with a small team covering design, production, the shop and the marketing between them.

The problem. The constraint in a small brand is never ideas. It is that a handful of people are covering a dozen jobs, and several of those jobs are being done badly because there is no time left in the day. Design stalls because writing the product listings ate the evening.

What we built. AI into the production work rather than around it — pushing design variations quickly enough to actually choose between them, drafting the listing copy so it sounds like the brand instead of like a template, and making the supporting pictures a launch needs.

What it gave back. The evenings, and with them the taste. The difference between a brand that launches and one that stays an idea is usually just whether the dull half of the work got done.

Independent game development

Three games shipped, because debugging stopped eating the week

An independent game studio building and releasing on a platform with millions of players, where the competition includes teams with actual payrolls.

The problem. Shipping a game means writing code, designing systems, making art, testing, then doing the whole thing again when players find the edges. For a small studio, speed is the entire business, and the hours lost to debugging are hours not spent on what makes the game good.

What we built. AI into the development loop — writing and debugging game code, working through systems design, and shortening the distance between an idea and something playable.

What it gave back. Three titles released. Shipping three times is how you learn what works, and a studio that can move at the pace of a larger team gets that many more attempts at being right.
